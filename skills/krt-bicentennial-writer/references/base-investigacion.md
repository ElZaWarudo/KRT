# Research Basis

Use this reference to explain why the skill prioritizes editorial quality, transparency, and context over promises to “beat detectors.”

## Operational Findings

- AI-text detectors are probabilistic and can produce false positives. Do not make their output the primary goal of a rewrite.
- Evidence shows that AI detectors can be biased against non-native English speakers; avoid diagnosing formal, regular, or second-language writing as “AI.”
- Vocabulary research shows abnormal increases in certain words and formulas in text published after widespread LLM adoption. Treat them as weak signals, not proof.
- Editorial and publication guidelines often require transparency about AI use when it affects authorship, writing, or research. Preserve disclosure guardrails.
- The best practical improvement is not to introduce mistakes, but to add specificity: real data, constraints, tradeoffs, examples, authorial voice, and visible decisions.

## Source Basis

- OpenAI retired its text classifier because of low accuracy and has recommended caution with automated detectors: https://openai.com/blog/new-ai-classifier-for-indicating-ai-written-text
- Stanford research found that GPT detectors incorrectly classified much of the writing by non-native speakers as AI-generated: https://hai.stanford.edu/news/ai-detectors-biased-against-non-native-english-writers
- Turnitin documents false positives and warns that its indicators must be reviewed alongside the context of the work: https://guides.turnitin.com/hc/en-us/articles/22774058814093-Using-the-AI-Writing-Report
- ICMJE treats AI as assistive technology that requires human accountability and transparent disclosure in manuscripts: https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/artificial-intelligence/ai-use-by-authors.html
- Recent bibliometric studies observe vocabulary changes associated with LLMs; these can help with stylistic audits but are insufficient for attribution: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40601754/
